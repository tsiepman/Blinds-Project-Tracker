The Model is not just a label. It is the one thing that ties our three systems together, so getting it right at creation saves a lot of unpicking later.

SystemWhat it does with the Model
D-ToolsWhat you pick when you spec a job
QuickBooksBecomes the item SKU — what gets invoiced
Pull LogWhat the installer scans when product leaves the shop

One product entered twice means a job invoices off two items and the numbers never reconcile. Two products sharing a stock code means the Orders page shows the wrong stock, or none at all. And once QuickBooks has transactions against an item it cannot simply be merged away.

1 Search before you create

Most catalog problems are a product that already existed. Before creating anything, search three ways:

  1. The manufacturer's part number
  2. The part number without hyphens or spaces
  3. Two or three words from the product name

If you find it, use it. If something about it is wrong, fix that product — don't create a second one beside it.

2 The Model is the part number, nothing else

Type it exactly as the manufacturer prints it. Keep their hyphens and their spacing. Don't add anything.

Don'tDoWhy

Hard limits, because QuickBooks enforces them

  • No colon (:) — QuickBooks reserves it for sub-items and will reject the item
  • No quote marks — write inches as IN or leave them out
  • Under 100 characters
  • No double spaces, no leading or trailing space
  • Two Models must differ by more than punctuation or capitalisation

Cable lengths: write them the way RepairQ does

RepairQ already numbers these properly — its manufacturer SKU for the 0.3 m AVPro cable is ACBT10KUHD003. Tenths of a metre, zero-padded. So copy RQ's digits rather than inventing anything.

003M

So AC-BT-10KUHD-003M, not AC-BT-10KUHD .3M.

Why pad at all: 1.5M and 15M are the same string once punctuation is stripped — and punctuation does get stripped, both by the duplicate check on the catalog and by the near-match prompt in the Pull Log. A decimal length is a part number that will eventually be taken for a different product. Padded digits can't collide.

Pad to the width the longest cable in that family needs. Three digits covers 0.1 m to 99.9 m, which is nearly everything. Blustream's HDMI18G range runs to 100 m, so that family needs four — HDMI18G-0010M through HDMI18G-1000M. Pick the width once per family and use it for every member, or renaming the 10 m collides with the existing 100 m.

This applies only where a length is genuinely part of the identifier. AT-UHD-PRO3-88M is Atlona's model number for the 8×8 matrix, not an 88 metre cable — leave that kind alone.

3 Fill in every field

A product created with only a Model and a price is a product someone has to come back to.

FieldWhat goes in it

PartNumber is the one people skip

The Model is ours; PartNumber is the manufacturer's. Put RepairQ's manufacturer SKU in it, character for character — ACBT10KUHD003.

It matters because the Orders page matches a product to its stock figure by RepairQ code first and PartNumber second. So when a code is wrong, missing, or shared between two products, PartNumber is what saves it.

CustomField1 (the RepairQ code) is retiring on January 1, 2027. RepairQ is switched off when the new company starts trading. After that stock comes from QuickBooks and the match is QuickBooks SKU straight to D-Tools Model — no separate code to keep in step.

Two things follow. Don't spend time tidying RepairQ codes — it's cleanup on a system being turned off; fix one only where a missing stock figure is costing an order now. And the Model is the identifier that survives, so time spent getting Models right pays off permanently.

Until then, the RepairQ code rules still apply

  • Leave it empty unless the product is genuinely stocked.
  • Use the real RepairQ code — never a quantity, a dash, or a guess. Values like 1.00 and 2.00 are in the catalog today and do nothing but break things.
  • Never put the same code on two products. If you do, both lose their stock figure — neither can be trusted to mean the other's stock, so the Orders page shows ?.

4 Who owns the catalog

The person who reviews the weekly report and fixes or reassigns what's on it:

Anyone may still create a product when they need it to quote — this plus the weekly report is the backstop, not a request queue that holds up a quote. But without a named owner the catalog drifts back within a few months. That is how it reached 5,400 products with 61 duplicate stock codes.

5 The weekly check

The project board sync produces a catalog report on request. In GitHub: Actions → Project board sync → Run workflow, tick Catalog report, then download catalog-issues.csv from the Artifacts box at the bottom of the run.

Each row names the product, the problem, and what to do. Two columns to read first:

  • Severity — blocker is worth fixing now, warn is worth fixing, info is a look
  • Holds import — yes means this product won't go into QuickBooks until it's fixed

How to work it

  1. Duplicates first. They're the only ones that corrupt invoicing, and they get harder to untangle as more jobs are quoted against both halves.
  2. Then the warnings, batched by manufacturer. Fixing every Lutron product in one sitting beats hopping around the catalog.
  3. Re-run the report. The blocker count going down is the measure. Fixed items drop off on their own — it's generated fresh each time, not a list anyone maintains.
The Model is the join key across D-Tools, QuickBooks and the Pull Log. Everything on this page exists to keep it unique and readable.
